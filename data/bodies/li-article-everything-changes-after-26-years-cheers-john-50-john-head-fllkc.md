1. The Move That Mattered

Jamie Dimon's $2 billion number stopped working for me.

I used the $2 billion AI figure Dimon gave Bloomberg News last October in a lot of client conversations: two billion in spend against two billion in savings, which he called the tip of the iceberg. I stopped using it this summer because everything around the number moved. JPMorganChase an set a $19.8 billion technology budget in February, and when Mike Mayo asked for the ROI on the AI piece, Dimon would not give one. On the July earnings call he told analysts AI will not expand margins because his competitors get the same tools. His CFO then put token spend on the table as a real cost line that is small today and expected to accelerate in the back half of the year.

The data point I use now is the four hours a week JPMorgan's own people say the internal model saves them, across 150,000 of the bank's 300,000 employees. The bank runs about 1,000 use cases and Dimon counts 50 of them as the ones that matter.

Why it matters: a 2,000 person company can plan around four hours a week per employee, and nobody can plan around $2 billion. Before your 2027 budget locks, find out where AI spend lands in it and who owns that line item.

2. What's Actually Shipping

I found two production programs in August that can show their number, and one vendor launch that arrived with named customers.

Salesforce Agentforce. Agentforce reached $1.2 billion in annual recurring revenue last quarter, up 205% year over year, on 3.8 billion completed tasks. Seven of Salesforce's top ten deals added seats, which tells me customers are buying more after they have watched it run.

Travelers built its own model. The $49 billion insurer trained TravelersLLM on millions of internal documents and now routes each query to that model or to a frontier model by task type. EVP Mojgan Lefebvre told CIO Dive that the decade of data modernization behind it is what made building cheaper than buying.

Google shipped Gemini Enterprise with references attached. The financial services edition launched with Deutsche Bank as design partner and CME Group as an early adopter. The legal edition launched with Cleary Gottlieb Steen & Hamilton LLP, Freshfields and Weil, Gotshal & Manges LLP signed on.

The pattern I see: McKinsey & Company's new research credits the companies that narrowed to a few workflows over the ones running dozens of pilots at once. Salesforce and Travelers both scoped the work down to something countable.

3. The Risk I'm Watching

Almost every large company has an AI governance policy, and a quarter of them cannot see the agents it is supposed to cover.

EY's new AI Risk and Governance Survey covered 202 senior AI executives at companies with at least $1 billion in revenue, and 91% of them now run agentic AI. The data point that jumped off the page for me is that 98% have formal governance policies while 26% cannot detect unauthorized AI agents running inside their own company. I see the skipped-process pattern constantly at large companies, where the governance board meets monthly and the business deploys weekly. A policy that gets bypassed under deadline pressure is a suggestion.

The numbers from the EY survey:

98% have formal AI governance policies
26% cannot detect unauthorized AI agents inside the company
Nearly half skipped the governance process for at least one urgent deployment
More than a third have had an AI incident cause material harm
64% of the companies that ran a formal assurance review significantly modified a quarter or more of their AI systems

IBM's 2026 Tech Leader Study of 2,000 technology executives says the same thing from the CIO's chair: 66% are held accountable for AI systems they do not fully control, and 11% feel fully prepared for the agent scale they expect. I wrote a longer piece on that study in August, "Accountable for AI you cannot see" (https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/accountable-ai-you-cannot-see-john-head-qrctc/).

What to do: run one review on the agents already in production before you approve any new ones. Pull the inventory from your logs and expense data, then put a named owner and a monthly cost next to every line.

4. Microsoft and Big Tech Watch

Issue 03 covered the model releases. The recap I posted in mid-July was about what the four big vendors wrapped around those models, and every one of them now sells an agent as the flagship enterprise product.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Work. It shipped alongside GPT-5.6 on July 9 as the agent that gathers context across connected apps and delivers the finished document. Enterprise context went to 1.5M tokens, 3.75x the GPT-5.5 ceiling.

Anthropic repriced Fable 5 and widened Cowork. Claude Fable 5 moved to usage-based credits on July 8 at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output, less than half of Mythos Preview pricing. Claude Cowork expanded to web and mobile and added Microsoft 365 write tools that draft email and update OneDrive and SharePoint.

Google took Gemini 3.5 Flash to GA. It is generally available across Global, US and EU regions, and Google reports 1.3 quadrillion total monthly tokens processed.

Microsoft made Copilot Cowork generally available. Claude is also a model option inside Copilot Chat now, the first big product result of the Microsoft and OpenAI exclusivity ending in April.

What does this mean for your roadmap: if your AI vendor strategy is written for a single frontier lab, it is already behind. I would spread the investment across more than one model the same way most of you spread workloads across hybrid cloud. Pick one production workflow that would benefit from being multi-model this quarter and name who owns that decision.

5. The Human Layer

Meta tried to trade headcount for agents at scale, and it stopped.

Reuters published the Project OT story in late August. Meta planned to shrink many teams by up to 60% and move the daily work to AI agents supervised by small human groups. Mark Zuckerberg halted the second wave of layoffs in May after the agents missed the productivity gains Meta had budgeted, employee sentiment scores fell from 74 to 55, and investors pushed back on the AI spend behind the plan. Meta is the biggest company yet to try that trade and fail, at least so far.

The Wakefield Research survey I wrote about in July explains some of it. Wakefield asked 300 C-level executives at US companies with at least $500 million in revenue, and 74% admitted they projected more confidence in their AI strategy than they privately felt. 79% would fundamentally change how they invested in AI if they could start over. What really stuck with me is that 88% said governance, risk or ethics concerns halted an initiative that looked financially attractive.

My take is that Meta had the budget and the agents and ended the program because they output didn't match the targets. Wholesale AI efforts are struggling where targeted programs deliver, and the difference I keep seeing is people who know the workflow deciding where the agent fits. When was your AI strategy last honestly reviewed, and what did you change as a result?
