# Magnifica Humanitas for the Enterprise AI Decision-Maker

2026-05-28 | https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/magnifica-humanitas-enterprise-ai-decision-maker-john-head-vqodc/

I'm a practicing Polish Catholic. I sell enterprise AI for a living. This week those two parts of my life came head to head.

Pope Leo XIV released his first encyclical on Monday. It is 42,000 words,  235 pages entitled Magnifica Humanitas, "the grandeur of humanity." The subject is artificial intelligence. The release coincided with the 135th anniversary of Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum, the 1891 letter that shaped the Church's response to industrialization. Leo XIV is drawing the parallel on purpose. AI is to our era what the factory was to 1891.

For a Polish Catholic the lineage is personal. John Paul II, Karol Wojtyła, worked in a chemical plant before he became a priest. He wrote Laborem Exercens in 1981 on the dignity of human work, and Leo XIV quotes him directly. One line in particular: "Fair wages are the concrete means of verifying the justness of the entire socioeconomic system because they reveal whether the worker is treated as a person or merely as a cost of production." Swap "fair wages" for "AI deployment plan" and you have the question every Fortune 500 board should be asking right now.

Leo XIV's core argument is one I have been making in client meetings all year, and I am glad to have a 2,000-year-old institution back me up. Technology is not neutral. It "takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it" (¶9). The model, the prompt, the agent and everything else connected carry your fingerprints. So does the deployment. If you build a system that quietly evaluates people by output alone, that is the system you built. The encyclical is direct about it: persons "should never be instrumentalized" and must not be "evaluated principally according to the outcomes they produce" (¶94).

Some of what he asks of enterprise leaders reads like a board agenda. Transparency about algorithms. Equitable access to data. Independent checks and avenues for recourse (¶71). Treating data, patents, platforms, and infrastructure as goods with a universal destination, rather than as private moats (¶67). No handful of actors setting the rules of digital life unilaterally (¶72). Honesty about both the hype and the fear (¶14).

He hands the enterprise a single evaluation question for any AI investment. From ¶85: "Do they truly help individuals and peoples to become more humane and fraternal, while respecting our common home and future generations?" We should run every business case and workforce plan through that question. If the answer is no, the deployment will pay you back in costs that have not shown up in the model yet.

A detail nobody in this industry should miss. Pope Leo broke tradition and invited Christopher Olah, co-founder of Anthropic , to speak at the encyclical's release. Olah said the quiet part out loud: frontier AI labs sit inside incentive structures that can conflict with doing the right thing, and the world needs voices outside those incentives who are willing to say hard things. The Pope agreed by putting him on stage. That a pontiff and an AI founder shared a microphone to say the same thing tells you what kind of moment we are in. It’s also a direct contrast with how the church handled Galileo and so many other scientists at the time. Pope John Paul II apologized for errors in the Galileo Trial in 1992 emphasized the need for dialogue between science and faith. Pope Leo’s enclitical takes acknowledging the role that science plays in the world by the church to a whole different level.

This is personal for me. I have thought for a long time about how my faith and my career sit next to each other. For most of my career the answer felt simple. Work hard. Sell well. Take care of clients. Go to mass. Be a good person. Do good deeds. AI changed that math. The systems I am selling now make decisions about people. They affect work, wages, attention, and dignity in ways the typical development project did not. The Pope is telling people like me, by name, that this work is not morally neutral, that we carry responsibility for what gets built, and that the standard is whether the result makes humans more human or less.

That is uncomfortable. It is also the right framing. The encyclical asks for honesty about the choice we are making. Build Babel, the tower of efficiency and monopoly that flattens difference. Or rebuild Jerusalem, the city of shared work and plural voices, where the person stays at the center.

Every CIO, CFO, and Chief AI Officer I work with is making that choice right now, whether they know it or not. Read the encyclical. Bring up the topics explored within at your next AI steering committee. Pope Leo just handed you the best framework for AI governance I have seen written in any language this year, and he wrote it for free.

One last quote I cannot let go of. In paragraph 213 the Pope cites Gandalf. From The Lord of the Rings - The Return of the King: "It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till." Quoting Tolkien in an encyclical puts a fictional wizard's counsel into official Catholic teaching. It’s a full circle moment as John Ronald Reuel Tolkien was a devout Catholic. The choice was deliberate, and the line lands harder for those of us in technology because Tolkien lives in our cultural mindset - The Return of the King won 11 Academy Awards, including Best Picture. The Pope's point is small and direct. We are not asked to control the whole arc of AI. We are asked to tend our own field, uproot what is evil there, and leave clean earth for the people who come after. That is the work.
