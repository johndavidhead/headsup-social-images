# Your AI state of the union, and what to do for the rest of the year

2026-06-24 | https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/your-ai-state-union-what-do-rest-year-john-head-wzroc/

Let's run a thought experiment.

You sit on the board, or the executive team, of a mid-market company. Call it somewhere between $100 million and $1 billion in revenue. Real scale, real margins, no infinite budget.

You brought me in for one thing. A straight state of the union on your AI progress, and a plan for what to do through the rest of the year.

So here it is. No hype. Just what the data says and what I would do if it were my company.

You are worried about three things

I can guess them, because every team your size has the same three.

You think you are behind. You are watching the AI bill climb every month. And you have already spent real money on pilots that returned nothing.

All three are rational. None of them is a reason to slow down. Each one tells you to change how you buy, build, and govern AI, starting now.

Let me take them in order.

"Are we behind?"

Probably not the way you fear.

Adoption is close to universal. S&P Global's survey shows companies pushing AI deeper into operations and expecting returns, not running science experiments. The whole field is past the question of whether to use AI.

So your competitors are spending. Spending more is the easy part.

Here is the reframe. Being ahead does not mean spending the most. It means landing in the 6% McKinsey calls high performers, the companies that can point to real EBIT from AI. You get into that group with discipline, not with budget.

And a company your size can get there faster than a giant can. You have fewer layers between a decision and a redesigned workflow. The thing to worry about is not your spend. It is your execution.

"Our pilots failed"

You are not the exception here. You are the median.

MIT studied more than 300 enterprise AI deployments in 2025. Ninety-five percent of organizations got zero measurable return on their generative AI spend. Companies put $30 to $40 billion into the technology and most of it never reached the P&L.

That is not a verdict on the technology. MIT named the cause a "learning gap." The models work. The integration into real workflows, real teams, and real incentives is what breaks down.

The 5% that succeeded shared one habit. They redesigned the work around the tool. They did not bolt the tool onto a process that was already broken.

S&P Global saw it from the other side. The share of companies that abandon most of their AI initiatives jumped from 17% to 42% in a single year. The average company now scraps 46% of its proof-of-concept projects before they ever reach production.

So when your pilots stall, you are in a very large crowd. The good news is the failure has a known shape. And a known fix.

"The costs keep climbing"

This one confuses most CFOs, so stay with me.

The price of AI is falling fast. The bill is going up anyway.

The blended cost of a model dropped about 67% in a year, from $18.40 to roughly $6 per million tokens. Volume grew faster than anyone budgeted. Seventy-three percent of enterprises reported AI costs that ran past their original projections, some at three times plan. Uber burned through its 2026 AI coding budget in four months and capped tools at $1,500 per person per month.

The driver is agents. One agent makes many model calls per task and drags a growing context along, so it eats 10 to 50 times the tokens of a single chat. Cheaper per unit. Far more units. Run that with no meter and the budget breaks.

So the fear should not be "AI is too expensive." It should be "we have no cost discipline on AI." Those are different problems. The first has no fix. The second you already know how to solve, the same way finance tamed cloud spend with FinOps.

You do not need to wait for a standard. You need a budget, a cap, and an owner on every AI workflow this quarter.

What the winning 5% actually do

McKinsey puts hard numbers on the gap. Only 39% of companies report any enterprise-level EBIT impact from AI, and most of those see less than 5%.

The single strongest predictor of impact is fundamental workflow redesign. High performers are 3.6 times more likely to pursue transformational change, and roughly 55% rework the workflow when they deploy, against about 20% for everyone else.

The losers automate the old process. The winners change the process. That is the whole game.

Two more things separate them.

They buy instead of build. MIT found that buying from specialized vendors and partners succeeds about 67% of the time, while internal builds succeed at about a third of that rate. A company your size has no reason to build a foundation model. Buy the capability. Own the workflow.

And they look in the back office, not the customer-facing activities. Document processing, invoice handling, and compliance reporting are the highest-ROI targets in 2026, with payback often under a year. Most AI budgets chase sales and marketing, where impact is hard to measure. Most of the actual return sits in the repetitive, high-volume work that finance and operations run every day. Clean baseline. Clear owner. A number you can show a board.

Now the plan. Q3, today through September.

This quarter is cleanup plus a few disciplined bets.

Kill the zombie pilots. Any project that cannot name a baseline and a dollar outcome gets stopped now. You are already scrapping half your proofs of concept. Do it early and on purpose.

Pick the work, not the tool. Choose two or three narrow, high-volume, structured workflows. Invoice processing. Claims intake. Vendor onboarding. Contract review. Write down today's cost, cycle time, and error rate first. No baseline, no project.

Buy before you build. Default to proven tools for the capability. Spend your team's effort on integration and on redesigning the workflow around the tool, which is the part that drives return.

Put a meter on it. Token budgets per workflow, caps per team, a monthly cost-per-outcome report to finance. Treat AI spend the way you treat cloud spend, because it now behaves the same way.

Stand up governance now. Name an accountable owner for each system. Put a human review gate on anything that touches money or customers. Keep a simple register of what AI you run and why.

Q4, October through December.

This quarter is for scaling the winners and proving the number.

Redesign first, then scale. Take the one or two workflows that beat their baseline and rebuild the whole process around the tool, including the human steps. That is where the EBIT comes from. Scaling a half-fixed process just multiplies the friction.

Match the model to the job. Do not run your most expensive model on routine work. Route simple, high-volume tasks to cheaper models. Reserve the powerful ones for work that was impossible before. The model choice is a budget decision, not a technical one.

Measure in EBIT terms. By year end, management reports AI the way it reports any other investment. Dollars saved. Hours returned. Cycle time cut. Risk reduced. If a workflow cannot show that, it goes back to the lab or gets cut.

Move one workflow to agentic, with guardrails. Pick a single bounded process where an agent can run a multi-step task end to end, inside hard cost caps, with a human approving the output. Learn the operating model on one before you turn agents loose across the business.

Beyond the year, the posture for 2027

Plan on the assumption that your competitors are deploying, not piloting.

Gartner expects 40% of enterprise applications to embed task-specific agents by the end of 2026, and more than 40% of agentic projects to be canceled by the end of 2027. Both are true at once. The cancellations will hit the companies that chased hype with no cost control and no governance. The survivors built the discipline in first.

Governance is the gap that decides which group you are in. Agentic AI is already in production at most enterprises, yet only about 21% have a mature governance model. For a mid-market company that gap is an opening. You can put real accountability and human oversight in place faster than a giant can, and that becomes a reason customers and regulators trust you.

The regulatory clock is running too. The EU AI Act's transparency rules carry an active 2026 date, with the heavier high-risk obligations landing at the end of 2027. Build the register, the owners, and the review gates this year and those dates become a formality instead of a fire drill.

The way I think about all of it

The work splits into three jobs that never stop. Advise: pick the right problems and design the workflow. Deploy: integrate and redesign the process around the tool. Run: govern it, meter it, and measure it every day.

The companies that treat all three as ongoing disciplines end up in the 5%. The ones who treat AI as a one-time software purchase end up in the 95%.

So, board member, here is your homework

Hold management to a short scorecard every quarter:

Cost per outcome for each AI workflow, trending down.
Hard-dollar or EBIT impact per workflow, against a real baseline.
Pilots started, scaled, and killed, so you can see the pruning.
Governance status for each production system: owner, review gate, compliance.

If management cannot fill in that table, you do not have an AI program. You have an AI expense.

And the one question I would put on the agenda for December: which two workflows will you point to and say, in dollars, that AI paid for itself?

Pick them now. Tell me in the comments which two you would choose.
