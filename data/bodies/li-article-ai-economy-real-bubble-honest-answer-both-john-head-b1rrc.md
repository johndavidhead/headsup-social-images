# Is the AI economy real, or a bubble? The honest answer is both.

2026-07-09 | https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-economy-real-bubble-honest-answer-both-john-head-b1rrc/

Every board and executive I sit with asks a version of the same question. Is this real, or are we about to look foolish?

Exponential View published a serious attempt at an answer in June. Their report, "The State of the AI Economy," rebuilds the demand side of AI from company filings, audited accounts, and earnings transcripts across more than a thousand firms, then strips out the double counting so a dollar that moves from an app to a model provider to a hosting bill gets counted once, not three times.

One caveat before the numbers. This is one firm's model, not audited fact. I read it as the best available map, not gospel. I checked it against other sources, and I want to give you both the bull case and the bear case, because your budget depends on holding both.

The demand is real, and fast

Generative AI revenue is running at a 175 billion dollar annualized pace, with 110 billion already banked over the past year. That is paying customers, not pilots. Exponential View measures it growing about three times faster than any prior tech wave. In 2023 the industry needed 180 days to add a billion dollars of cumulative revenue. It now needs under two days.

You can see the same pull in the physical world. The largest data centers have grown 50 times in four years. US electricity generation sat flat from 2008 to 2024 and is now adding about 9 terawatt-hours a month. This is a capital and power story before it is a software story.

And still a rounding error

Here is the number I put in front of any executive who thinks they are late. All of that AI revenue equals about 0.42 percent of US GDP. Corporate profits alone are 32 times larger than every dollar of generative AI revenue combined.

That cuts both ways. The bull reads it as room to run, since AI against GDP has grown tenfold in two years off almost nothing. The bear reads it as two years and hundreds of billions in spend for a sliver of measured output and thin proof of return. Both are fair. Early means unproven.

The buildout is clearing the low bar

Hyperscalers and newer AI cloud providers have committed to roughly two trillion dollars of capital spending through 2026. Is revenue covering the bill?

It is starting to clear one line. In the fourth quarter of 2025, quarterly AI infrastructure revenue passed the depreciation charge on that infrastructure. Read that carefully, because it oversells easily. Depreciation is not the whole cost. It leaves out power, staff, and model training. The report is direct that depreciation alone still eats about 81 percent of hyperscaler and neocloud AI revenue before those other costs land. The buildout is covering its accounting depreciation, not proving a profit.

The financing is the piece I watch closest. Hyperscalers fund their spend mostly with cash. The newer players fund theirs mostly with debt. AI firms tapped debt markets for more than 200 billion dollars in 2025, and analysts are tracking circular deals where chipmakers fund the same neoclouds that then buy their chips. When the marginal dollar of a buildout this size moves to outside lenders, the risk moves outside the companies too.

Falling prices grow the market

This is the finding I would attach to every budget conversation. Cheaper AI does not shrink the market. It expands it faster than the price drops. Exponential View finds that cutting the price of tokens raises usage more than enough to lift total spend. Sam Altman put the cost of a given level of AI falling about 10 times every 12 months. Sundar Pichai said Google went from 9.7 trillion tokens a month to over 480 trillion.

One caveat. The report's elasticity is measured over time, not from a clean price experiment, so it may overstate how much of the growth is price versus the technology simply getting better. The direction is clear even if the number is soft.

For a CIO the lesson is practical. Do not budget AI as a fixed line that gets cheaper. Budget it as a line that gets cheaper per unit and bigger in total, because your teams will find new uses faster than the price falls.

The case against

Now the part the report cannot tell you, because it measures supply-side revenue, not whether your company will see a return.

The enterprise track record is poor. Massachusetts Institute of Technology 2025 study of over 300 deployments found 95 percent of organizations got zero measurable return on their generative AI spend. The cause was integration into real workflows and teams, not the models.

McKinsey & Company 's 2025 survey lands in the same place. Adoption is near universal, but only about 6 percent of companies qualify as high performers, able to trace 5 percent or more of their earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) to AI. Just 21 percent have redesigned any workflow around the tool. Spending is easy. Getting paid for it is not.

Treat the adopter gap with care too. Exponential View shows heavy AI spenders growing revenue far faster than non-adopters. That is correlation. High-growth firms buy more of everything, so it does not prove AI caused the growth.

The bear case is simple. Enormous supply-side spend, real usage, and most buyers still unable to show a dollar of return. That looks like the late stage of a hype cycle. It also looks like the early stage of a real platform shift. The data does not yet tell you which.

Where I land

The demand is real and compounding. The buildout economics are workable but thin, and the financing risk is drifting toward outside lenders. None of that guarantees your AI program returns a dollar, and the base rate says most do not.

The split I see is not believers versus skeptics. It is the 6 percent who redesigned the work and can point to EBIT, and everyone else running the tool alongside a broken process. The macro tailwind is real. It will not save a bad implementation.

If you are building your AI plan for the rest of the year, anchor it to two things. A clear outcome you can put a dollar on, and a usage budget that assumes volume grows faster than price falls.

Ask your team one question this week: which of our AI workflows can name the dollar it returns? The answer tells you whether you are in the 6 percent or paying to find out.
