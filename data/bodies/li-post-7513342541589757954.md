I’ve watched a lot of AI budgets get approved the same way in the last year; someone puts up a price per token, multiplies it by expected usage, the number gets put into the budget line item and the conversation moves on. The Wall Street Journal ran a piece yesterday that shows why that formula doesn’t work going forward.

Researchers from Stanford University, Carnegie Mellon University, University of California, Berkeley and Microsoft Research ran more than 6,800 tasks and found the cheaper model cost more about a third of the time. In one test, Google's lighter Gemini model spent $14 on a job it never finished. The premium model did it for $1. Run the same prompt twice and you get two different bills, and you pay for the failed attempts too. No wonder only 11% of companies in a recent study can forecast their AI spend. 

My view is that the thought process for anyone budgeting AI for next year is what AI costs per finished piece of work, by team, with a cap on it. We already manage freight and energy this way, and AI spend belongs in the same conversation. 

These conversations are going to happen at every level this quarter as budgets for 2027 get locked down. Boards and Executives alike will be wondering what their AI spend will be, as what was budgeted for 2026 blew out estimates. Most companies had their budgets wrong quarter over quarter, let alone for the year. The only wise move is putting a cap on the spend. That means model diversity, local models vs. the premium frontier models, and being more specific about AI usage and outcomes are all conversations that should be going on right now.
